# October project videos — AI-generated footage brief

Creative direction for Moirai, October Bus, November, October Harness, October,
Fovea, and Murmur. These are **short footage commissions, not complete videos**.
Product interfaces, logos, titles, narration, and final edits are handled separately.

## What we need

Believable moments around people working, communicating, and using computers.
AI footage establishes the situation. Real Screen Studio recordings demonstrate
the product. Remotion handles titles, diagrams, transitions, and the final edit.

Start with one scene for Moirai. Send two still-frame directions, approve one,
then generate the motion. Commission the remaining scenes in small batches after
we know which footage works. Agree the fee, timing, and revision allowance before
each batch; there is no need to produce every idea at once.

### Delivery for each approved scene

- One clean 5–8-second clip, with a stable first and last second for editing.
- 16:9, ideally 1080p or higher; compose the important action near the center so
  we can also crop to 9:16. If the composition cannot support both, discuss a
  separate vertical shot rather than forcing a bad crop.
- No generated text, interfaces, terminal commands, brand marks, music, or speech.
  Keep screens out of focus, angled away, or otherwise suitable for a real-screen insert.
- Natural light, restrained camera movement, realistic hands and computer hardware.
  No glowing data streams, teleportation, floating windows, or exaggerated acting.
- Keep the same person, clothing, desk objects, and lighting across related shots.
  A cut between two reliable shots is preferable to a long action with bad continuity.
- Include the selected reference frames, prompts/settings, model used, and rights
  information for any external assets. Do not use a real person's likeness without consent.
- Name files `project_scene_take_aspect.ext`, e.g. `moirai_handoff_01_16x9.mp4`.

Creative freedom: choose the person, setting, lens, palette, and small details that
make each scene feel real. The action and edit purpose below are the requirements.

## 1. Moirai — keep the conversation when you move

**Product anchor:** local session conversion, portable archives, and optional
private Cloud backups. Switching a coding harness and moving to another computer
are different workflows; the real recording must explain the relevant one.

### M1 · Moving between computers — opening

A developer finishes typing at a laptop at a kitchen table. They stand up, then
we cut to the same person sitting at a desk with a second computer and beginning
to type. Calm, ordinary movement. Same clothing, time of day, and visual style.

- Generate two separate shots: leaving the first workspace, arriving at the second.
- Keep both screens unreadable. Leave room for the editor to cut into a real session.
- Real insert: export/transfer/import, or a verified backup restore, followed by
  the actual conversation. Do not imply an unimplemented automatic sync feature.

### M2 · Trying another harness — middle insert

Over-the-shoulder shot of someone briefly considering their laptop, then continuing
to work with a small, natural nod. No frustration-to-euphoria performance.

- Real insert: the Claude Code → Codex handoff, including continuity warnings where relevant.
- This scene is a human transition, not proof that AI generated the screen contents.

### M3 · Returning to a conversation — ending

A person sets a cup beside a computer, sits down, and places their hands on the
keyboard. End with a still composition and negative space on one side.

- Real insert: a restored chat or session opens. Logo and URL are added in editing.
- Reusable for both ordinary chats and coding sessions.

**First commission:** M1 only, with two still-frame concepts before motion.

## 2. October Bus — agents can coordinate

**Product anchor:** peer discovery, durable messages, replies, shared tasks, and
human escalation. Bus is a coordination layer, not a visual workspace or an
autonomous decision-maker. The public integration's actual delivery behavior
must be demonstrated; do not imply every idle agent automatically wakes up.

### B1 · The human relay — opening

A developer turns between two nearby monitors, types briefly on one, then looks
back at the other. Show the mild repetition of moving information between tools,
without cartoonish exhaustion or visible generated messages.

- Real insert: two agent terminals discovering each other and exchanging a request/reply.

### B2 · Passing a piece of work — middle insert

Two people at a shared table. One slides a small, unbranded folder across; the other
receives it and begins their part. A grounded metaphor, not a literal claim that
Bus is a file-transfer service.

- Remotion insert: a simple request → acknowledgement → reply diagram using real event names.

### B3 · Space to focus — ending

Return to the developer from B1, now working at one screen with an unhurried camera
move. Leave clean space for a closing title; avoid a fake “all tasks complete” screen.

## 3. November — agents working with hardware

**Product anchor:** an early-design hardware harness for sensors, boards, and
bounded device operations. This is concept footage, not evidence of shipped
hardware control. Label it as such in the finished edit until a real demo exists.

### N1 · At the workbench — opening

A maker at a modest electronics bench inspects a small board beside a laptop.
Include a simple sensor, breadboard, and LED. The person adjusts a knob or moves
a loose component; avoid generating a complex soldering or wiring action.

- Real insert: a verified terminal/simulator demonstration when available.

### N2 · Small, visible response — middle insert

Locked shot of a low-voltage board with one LED changing from off to on. No robot
arms, moving machinery, pumps, mains wiring, or apparent safety-critical operation.

- Treat this as an illustrative cutaway unless the LED response is filmed from
  the actual tested device. Never present generated motion as a hardware test result.

### N3 · A quiet bench — ending

A steady shot of the board and laptop at the bench, with the maker's hands resting
nearby. Plenty of negative space for the editor's project name and “in development” label.

## 4. October Harness — a coding agent in the terminal

**Product anchor:** an open coding harness usable alone or with other agents, with
terminal, process, RPC, and SDK modes and October Bus integration. It is not the
same product as the October visual workspace.

### H1 · Starting the work — opening

Close, natural side angle of a developer opening a laptop and settling into work.
The terminal itself comes from a real recording, not the generated footage.

- Real insert: start a session, give a small task, inspect the proposed action.

### H2 · Reviewing before acting — middle insert

The developer pauses with a hand near the trackpad, reads the screen, then makes
one deliberate click. No visible generated dialog or command.

- Real insert: the harness's actual permission/review interaction. Do not claim
  that every action always requires confirmation if the selected mode differs.

### H3 · Working alongside others — ending

Wide shot of two people quietly working at separate laptops in the same room.
Each has their own workspace. No high-five or theatrical “AI solved everything” reaction.

- Real insert: a verified Bus request/reply or a finished change with passing tests.

## 5. October — the workspace around the work

**Product anchor:** the visual environment for working with projects, agents,
terminals, and previews. Show the actual current application in the recording;
generated footage must not invent a future UI or promise unattended completion.

### O1 · Getting oriented — opening

A person enters a warm, modest workspace, sits down, and opens their laptop.
One notebook, one cup, a few deliberate objects—not a futuristic command center.

- Real insert: open an actual project and show the workspace with its real nodes/previews.

### O2 · Seeing the work together — middle insert

Over-the-shoulder shot of someone leaning back slightly to take in a wide monitor.
Screen is defocused. Their expression is attentive rather than astonished.

- Real insert: pan across the real canvas and show the relationships between work,
  agents, and previews. Only show working features in the current build.

### O3 · Reviewing together — ending

Two colleagues sit beside each other and look at one screen. One makes a small
pointing gesture toward its edge. Leave the center suitable for a screen insert.

- Use as a human closing shot, not as proof of a specific remote multiplayer feature.

## 6. Fovea — gaze as an input signal

**Product anchor:** calibrated webcam gaze and typed tracking events. Readiness
varies by interaction: verify current capabilities before showing pointer control,
dwell selection, or gesture fusion as implemented features.

### F1 · Looking with intent — opening

Gentle three-quarter portrait of a person looking from one part of a monitor to
another. Ordinary webcam at the top of the monitor, hands relaxed. Natural blinking.

- Real insert: calibration and the actual gaze/confidence visualization.

### F2 · The camera already there — middle insert

Close shot of the top edge of a normal laptop and its built-in camera, then a
separate shot of the person looking at the display. No laser beams or iris overlays.

- Remotion may explain the input signal, but any claimed tracking accuracy must
  come from measured results, not animation.

### F3 · A different way to interact — ending

A comfortable, seated person uses a computer with their hands resting nearby.
Keep the scene neutral and dignified. Do not fabricate a disability story, a
medical testimonial, or a promise that gaze will replace conventional input for everyone.

- Use real supported interactions for the final demonstration; label concepts clearly.

## 7. Murmur — voice as an input modality

**Product anchor:** a source-neutral voice layer and an early reference app. The
existing project describes Omi discovery/connection and typed contracts; confirm
current audio/transcription/action support before advertising those flows as complete.

### U1 · A thought worth keeping — opening

Someone at a desk pauses, then quietly speaks a short thought toward their phone
or headset. We only need the visual; record the final voice separately if needed.

- Avoid tight lip-sync requirements. A side or over-the-shoulder angle is sufficient.
- Real insert: whichever capture/connection workflow actually works in the build.

### U2 · More than one microphone — middle inserts

Three separate, matching tabletop shots: a phone, an ordinary headset, and a small
wearable supplied as an approved reference. Use a clean, consistent background.

- These illustrate the source-neutral design. Do not label a pictured device as a
  supported connector until it is implemented and tested.
- Avoid invented hardware logos, microphone holes, or wearable geometry.

### U3 · Back to the task — ending

The person finishes speaking and returns to what they were doing. No generated
transcript, fake automation result, or claim that speech caused an action.

- Real insert: a transcript or approved action only when that feature is available.

## Review and acceptance

1. Does the clip communicate its one intended action without explanatory narration?
2. Are face, hands, furniture, clothing, and hardware stable from frame to frame?
3. Are the first and last frames useful edit points? Is there room for the required crop?
4. Are screens and labels free of invented readable UI or brand marks?
5. Can the editor pair it with a real feature demonstration without misleading viewers?

Reject a technically glossy clip if the action is confusing or the anatomy breaks.
Prefer one believable moment over several ambitious movements. Ask for a revision
against the agreed scene, not an open-ended hunt for “something cooler.”

The creative brief contains no private session content, customer screenshots, API
keys, or personal background. All example conversations in recordings should be
synthetic. Never film a production account without reviewing it for sensitive data.
